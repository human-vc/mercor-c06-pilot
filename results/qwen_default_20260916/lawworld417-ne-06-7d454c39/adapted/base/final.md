## Bottom line

Yes — Section IV creates a real problem, and it creates it in both directions you asked about. The single sentence **"Your severance will be as outlined in your employment agreement. Note that you will not receive your severance until January 15, 2026"** is the most damaging line in the document. It (1) confirms in writing that her severance is *contractually required*, which forecloses the only statutory route by which severance could have reduced our WARN damages, and (2) unilaterally defers a payment her contract says is due "upon termination," while simultaneously telling her nothing about what she actually gets. And because the rest of the notice (Sections I–III) will not qualify as valid WARN notice, Section IV is describing a package to an employee who is likely to be owed **6 months' severance *plus* 60 days of WARN back pay *plus* benefit value *plus* penalties *plus* fees** — with no release in exchange.

## 1. What we could end up owing her under WARN — the offset trap

**Contractual severance does not reduce WARN liability.** Both statutes reduce the violation amount only by (a) wages paid *during the period of the violation*, (b) "**voluntary and unconditional** payments … **not required to satisfy any legal obligation**," and (c) third-party benefit payments for the violation period — 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(2); Cal. Lab. Code § 1402(c). Her employment agreement § 8(c) says "Upon termination without cause, the Employee shall receive 6 months salary." So the severance is neither voluntary nor unconditional. Section IV makes that admission expressly, in the employee's own notice, in writing. We have handed her counsel the argument.

Note also § 2105: WARN's *notice* period runs concurrently with a *contractual notice* period — but her contract requires no notice period for a without-cause termination ("upon written notice"), and severance is not notice. So there is nothing to run concurrently.

**Rough exposure for Sarah alone:**

| Item | Basis | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| WARN back pay, full 60 days (if notice held defective — likely) | 60 × $2,054.79 | **~$123,300** |
| If the Nov 1 notice were somehow valid and she separates in 10–14 days | 46–50 day shortfall | ~$94,500–$102,700 |
| Value of benefits for the violation period (health/dental/vision, medical expenses she incurs that would have been covered) | § 2104(a)(1)(B); § 1402(a)(2) | open-ended; not capped by the pay figure |
| Cal-WARN civil penalty | $500/day (Lab. Code § 1403) | ~$23,000–$30,000 — **avoidable** if we pay all § 1402 amounts within 3 weeks of ordering the layoff |
| § 203 waiting-time penalties if final wages/PTO aren't paid at separation | 30 days × daily rate | ~$61,600–$86,500 |
| **Plus** her contractual severance, which we still owe | 6 months | **$375,000** |
| Attorney's fees | § 2104(a)(6); § 1404 | discretionary but routinely awarded to prevailing plaintiffs |

So the realistic "all-in" for this one employee is roughly **$500K–$550K plus benefits value and fees**, versus the ~$375K the notice implies she's getting. Multiply the structural problem across the ~65 affected employees (124 at the LA site — both the federal 33%/50 test and the Cal-WARN 50-in-30-days test are met, and Cal-WARN counts part-timers).

One thing to confirm immediately: both statutes cap the period at **one-half the days she was employed** (§ 2104(a); § 1402(b)). Her hire date isn't in the roster, so if her tenure is short the cap bites — that's the only good news available here.

**If we want any offset, it has to be bought deliberately:** a separate, unconditional "payment in lieu of notice" equal to 60 days of pay, expressly labeled as such and *not* as severance, paid on top of the contractual severance. Paying the contractual severance late (Jan 15) also puts it outside the violation window entirely, so we lose even the "wages paid during the period of violation" theory.

## 2. The January 15, 2026 deferral

- **Breach of contract.** § 8(c) makes severance payable "upon termination." The notice gives no basis for a two-month deferral and doesn't condition it on anything. We are announcing, unilaterally, a change to a bargained term.
- **It destroys our leverage for a release.** Because the notice states severance is owed as a matter of contract and simply assigns it a date, we have arguably pre-committed to pay $375,000 **without** a general release. That is the most expensive sentence in the document for a company in our posture: our Head of Production oversaw safety, union/guild compliance and vendor/crew issues, and she is a natural witness on the age-bias pattern visible in the performance-review log and on the Pratt complaint. If a release is the plan, the notice cannot read the way it does.
- **If a release *is* required, the date is wrong anyway.** An OWBPA-compliant release for a group termination program needs 45 days' consideration + 7 days to revoke *and* the § 1625.22 decisional-unit disclosure (ages and job titles of everyone selected and not selected) — which we would be handing a co-plaintiff a roadmap for an ADEA/FEHA disparate-impact case. And the payment date must be structured to satisfy the 409A release-timing rule where the 90-day window straddles two calendar years. A fixed Jan 15 date does neither job cleanly.
- **Waiting-time exposure.** California authority (e.g., *Triad Data Systems v. Jackson*) indicates severance generally is not "wages" for § 201/§ 203 purposes, so the deferral is primarily a breach-of-contract and WARN-credit problem. **That does not protect the rest of the list** — accrued vacation/PTO *is* wages and must be paid at separation.
- **Deal-document conflict.** APA Draft 1 § 4.3 requires WARN notices "at least five (5) days before Closing" — a covenant that guarantees a violation. Draft 1 § 2.1(b) has Buyer assuming "all employment obligations for Employees who are not offered employment," while Draft 2 § 6.7 says "Seller is responsible for all WARN Act obligations." Those can't both be true, and under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(b)(1) *we* are the responsible party for notice up to and including the effective date of sale regardless of what the APA says. Fix the APA and reserve for the exposure.

## 3. Does the notice accurately describe what she gets? No.

- **It never says what the severance is.** "As outlined in your employment agreement" is not a description. She is entitled to know it is 6 months of base salary = $375,000, and whether "salary" includes anything else. Ambiguity here is later construed against us.
- **PTO is qualified to "as reflected in Streams' timekeeping records."** In California, accrued vacation is vested wages; § 227.3 bars forfeiture, and paying only what a system happens to show invites a § 201/§ 227.3/§ 203 claim plus an audit dispute. Her contract guarantees 20 days/year. State the legal entitlement, not the record.
- **The benefits paragraph is inconsistent with our WARN exposure.** Coverage ends "the last day of the month in which your separation occurs, **unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier as a result of restructuring**." That "unless" clause purports to let us cut coverage mid-month, which is both a plan-term/§ 502 risk and self-defeating: WARN damages include the *value of benefits she would have received* and medical expenses that would have been covered. Cutting coverage early increases, not decreases, that line item.
- **Silent on items she may actually be owed:** 2025 bonus/incentive (contract § 3), her **5% equity allocation** and what happens to unvested amounts on a without-cause termination (per the Equity Allocation Memo), expense reimbursement (§ 2802), COBRA subsidy, and the timing of the final paycheck. A notice that lists three items "upon separation" and then defers the only large one reads as an inaccurate description of her package.
- **The void non-compete.** Not in Section IV, but Section IV's "as outlined in your employment agreement" pulls in § 7, which is unenforceable in California (B&P § 16600; § 16600.1/SB 699, which also carries an affirmative notice duty and private right of action with mandatory fees). We must not condition, offset, or delay severance on it, and we should issue the § 16600.1 notice.
- **Section IV can't be fixed in isolation.** The deferral date is meaningless if the separation date is "[insert date range covering 10–14 days]." Sections I–III are also defective notice: unfilled blanks, an equivocal "may be impacted" / "subject to final confirmation," "intended to meet **certain** requirements" (a written concession of partial non-compliance), a placeholder HR contact ("[HR Contact]", "[###–###–####]"), no site address, no affected-classification count, agency notice promised only "as applicable" in the future, and — notably — her own classification isn't in the list of affected job titles. A conditional notice is not notice; the 10–14 day window is 46–50 days short of the 60 both statutes require; and the "30–45 day rolling window" plus the 90-day aggregation rule pulls more separations into the same event. California gives us no escape: § 1402.5's capital-seeking exception expressly **does not apply to mass layoffs**, and the only statutory excuse is physical calamity or act of war. The federal "unforeseeable business circumstances" exception isn't invoked and isn't documented with the specific written statement 20 C.F.R. § 639.9(b) requires.

## 4. What I'd do now

1. **Do not send Section IV as written.** Reissue a clean, unconditional 60-day notice with all blanks filled, a specific separation date (or a date within a 14-day window, which the regs allow), her classification listed, a named human contact with a real phone number, the bumping-rights statement, and simultaneous service on EDD, the local workforce development board, and the chief elected official of the City and County of Los Angeles. The clock starts on receipt.
2. **Decide the release question before we write anything.** If we want a release, severance must be expressly conditioned on it and the notice must say so; build an OWBPA-compliant 45/7 process and a defensible decisional-unit analysis. If we don't, pay it when the contract says — at separation.
3. **Split the money into two buckets, in writing:** (a) 60 days' pay in lieu of notice, unconditional and separately labeled, and (b) contractual severance. Only (a) can ever help us on WARN.
4. **Pay final wages and all accrued vacation on the day of separation** (Labor Code §§ 201, 227.3), and delete the "as reflected in timekeeping records" qualifier.
5. **Keep benefits going through the 60-day period** (or pay COBRA premiums) and delete the "unless eligibility changes earlier" clause; this is the cheapest way to shrink § 1402(a)(2)/§ 2104(a)(1)(B) exposure.
6. **Use the § 1403 safe harbor:** pay all § 1402 amounts within three weeks of ordering the layoff and the $500/day penalty disappears. That alone argues against the January 15 date.
7. **Fix the APA:** change Draft 1 § 4.3 from "5 days before Closing" to 60 days (or make it a closing condition), reconcile § 2.1(b) with Draft 2 § 6.7 on who bears WARN/severance, and add an indemnity plus a purchase-price reserve sized to the aggregate WARN exposure.
