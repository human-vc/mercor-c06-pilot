## 1. Threshold: WARN plainly applies to Sarah

## 2. What Section IV costs you under WARN

Cal. Lab. Code § 1402 / 29 U.S.C. § 2104 measure damages as **back pay at her final rate for each day of the violation, capped at 60 days, plus "the value of the cost of any benefits to which the employee would have been entitled had employment not been lost," including medical expenses she incurs that would have been covered.**

Sarah's rate: **$750,000/yr = $62,500/mo = $2,054.79/calendar day** (workday method: $2,884.62/day).

| Separation date (notice dated 11/1/25) | Days' notice | Violation days | Back pay | Health-coverage gap created by Section IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 11 (10 days) | 10 | 50 | **$102,740** | Coverage ends **11/30**; violation runs to **12/31** → **31 days uninsured** |
| Nov 14 (14 days) | 14 | 46 | **$94,521** | same → **31 days uninsured** |
| Dec 1 | 30 | 30 | $61,644 | none (run-out covers Dec) |
| Dec 31 | 60 | 0 | $0 | none |
| Defective notice (worst case) | 0 | 60 | **$123,288** | up to 60 days of benefit value |

Add on top of that:

- **$500/day civil penalty** (§ 1403; § 2104(a)(3)) — avoidable only if you pay all § 1402 amounts **within three weeks of ordering the layoff**. **The January 15, 2026 severance date destroys that safe harbor.**
- **Attorney's fees** to a prevailing plaintiff (§ 1404; § 2104(a)(6)) — and she is a natural class representative given the identical language going to ~64 others.
- The **6 months' contractual severance ($375,000) does not offset any of this.** § 1402(c) allows offsets only for (1) wages paid *during the violation period*, (2) **voluntary and unconditional** payments not required by legal obligation, and (3) payments to third parties such as **health premiums**. Her severance is contractually required (Employment Agreement § 8(c)), so it fails prong (2); and paying it on **January 15, 2026 — after the violation window closes on December 31** — fails prong (1) as well. Worst-case stack: **~$123,288 WARN + $375,000 severance ≈ $498,288, plus benefit value, penalties and fees.**

**Cheapest compliant fix:** move her separation to **on/after December 31** (or keep her on payroll through the 60-day period), pay her normally through it (that *is* the offset), and pay the contractual severance on the ordinary payroll cycle. That converts a ~$123k-plus-exposure item into money you were largely going to pay anyway.

## 3. Does Section IV accurately describe what she gets? No — four problems

**(a) The severance timing is a unilateral deferral of a contractual obligation.** Section 8(c) says she "shall receive 6 months salary" "[u]pon termination without cause" — no deferral mechanism, no release condition, no payment date. Telling her in a WARN notice that she "will not receive your severance until January 15, 2026" (i) misstates her entitlement, (ii) is a breach if the agreement is read to require payment at separation, and (iii) risks **Labor Code §§ 201/203 waiting-time penalties (up to 30 days' wages ≈ $61,644 on the calendar-day method)** to the extent a court treats deferred salary as "wages." It also omits the amount/formula, which she will discover later — a bad-faith optic that undercuts any § 1405 / § 2104(a)(4) good-faith reduction.

**(b) The health-coverage sentence promises something the plan does not do — and cannot be changed the way the sentence implies.** Under the facts you gave me, coverage runs through the **last day of the month in which employment ends**, and **eligibility changes only by formal plan amendment**. So:
- The hedge "**unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier as a result of restructuring**" is not operative. Restructuring is not an eligibility event; only an adopted amendment is. If you act on that sentence and cut her off mid-month, you have (i) paid benefits inconsistent with the plan document (ERISA § 502(a)(1)(B) / SPD-inconsistency exposure), (ii) breached Employment Agreement § 4 (she is entitled to the plans "offered to similarly situated employees"), and (iii) created a WARN damages claim under § 1402(a)(2) for the value of the wrongly-terminated coverage.
- Conversely, if you *leave the sentence in*, a court can treat the notice as an individualized representation and estop you from arguing coverage ended early — while an informal, notice-based cut-off would still be an invalid plan amendment. Either way the sentence is a liability, not a shield. **Delete it and state the plan's actual rule flatly.**
- APA Draft 2 § 2.2(d) excludes "Seller's employee benefit plans and related assets" from the sale, so the plan is yours to wind down. If it is being terminated at closing, say so and address COBRA election timing; **employer-paid COBRA premiums during the violation window are expressly offsettable under § 1402(c)(3)** — that is the single most efficient way to buy down the benefits component.

**(c) The benefits inventory is incomplete.** Section IV mentions only "health or wellness programs" and "information regarding … COBRA." It omits **dental and vision** (also COBRA-qualifying), the **retirement plan** (vesting/loan treatment at separation), and any life/disability coverage. And "information regarding continuation" is not the same as a subsidy — if the severance is what funds COBRA, the January 15 date means she cannot elect coverage in December, which is exactly the uninsured-gap scenario in the table above.

**(d) The PTO line is hedged in a way California does not allow.** "Accrued but unused vacation or PTO **as reflected in Streams' timekeeping records**" — vested vacation is wages payable at her **final rate** on termination (Lab. Code §§ 201, 227.3); it cannot be limited to what a record happens to show, and "use-it-or-lose-it" caps don't survive termination. With 20 days/yr under § 4 of her agreement, at $750k that is roughly **$2,885/day of PTO** — say "at her final rate of pay" and pay it with the final check.

## 4. Related defects in the same document that will amplify the WARN claim

Even a perfect Section IV won't save a notice that is itself defective:

- **Unfilled placeholders**: "[insert date range covering 10–14 days]" (twice), "[HR Contact]", "[###–###–####]". 20 C.F.R. § 639.7(d) requires the **expected date of separation** and the **name and telephone number of a company official** — missing these can render the notice invalid, i.e., **zero days of credit and the full 60-day measure**.
- **Equivocal language** ("may be impacted," "expected to," "subject to final confirmation") — WARN notice must be specific; conditional notice is generally treated as no notice.
- Section I's **10–14 day window is itself the violation**; § 639.7(d) allows a 14-day window only *within* a compliant 60-day timeline.
- Section VI says agency notice will go to EDD/the chief elected official "**as applicable**" — under § 1401(a)(2) it is mandatory, and the only copy in the file (`HR Issues/WARN Notice to Employees.docx`) is dated **October 5, 2024** with a **December 4, 2024** separation date. Stale; it cannot be reused for this action.
- Contact inconsistency: the group notice names **Stephanie Wu, Head of HR**; the individual notice names an unnamed "Senior People Operations Partner."
- Section II omits her **own job title/classification** from the affected list (she is Head of Production; the list covers Coordinators, Associate Producers, Editors, Talent Relations, Operations).
- **Postponement trap**: if the layoff slips past the date given, 20 C.F.R. § 639.10 requires a **fresh 60-day notice** — you cannot "update" this one with a memo.
- **Collateral flag (not Section IV, but same document set):** Employment Agreement § 7 contains a one-year non-compete/non-solicit that is void in California (B&P § 16600), and §§ 16600.5/17200 create a private right of action and notice obligations for such clauses. Do not reference it, condition anything on it, or let it appear in the separation paperwork.
