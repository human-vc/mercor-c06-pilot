**Bottom line**

Yes, Section IV creates two problems. First, the way it describes the severance guarantees that none of it can be credited against WARN liability, so if the timing slips you pay her six months' salary plus up to 60 days of WARN back pay and benefits on top. Second, the January 15, 2026 payment date is not in her agreement, and the section is silent on several things she is actually owed or might claim.

**1. WARN exposure: the severance language forecloses any offset**

Section IV says "Your severance will be as outlined in your employment agreement." Her agreement (Chasing Stream, LLC/Employment Agreements/Employment Agreement - Head of Production.docx, Section 8(c)) says: "Upon termination without cause, the Employee shall receive 6 months salary." At $750,000 a year, that is $375,000.

Both statutes reduce WARN back pay only by "voluntary and unconditional" payments "not required by any legal obligation" (29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(2)(B); Cal. Lab. Code § 1402(c)(2)). Contractual severance is a legal obligation. By labeling the payment as the contractual severance, the notice itself establishes that it earns zero credit. Delaying it to January 15 does not help either: the other offset, wages paid "for the period of the violation," requires her to be on payroll during that period, and a deferred lump sum is not that.

So if the notice fails (and as drafted it does: Section I has "[insert date range]" placeholders, says the date "may occur earlier or later," and calls her position one that "may be impacted"; a WARN notice needs a specific separation date or a 14-day window under 20 C.F.R. § 639.7, and the 60 days do not start running from a placeholder), the exposure on Sarah alone is roughly:

- $375,000 contractual severance (owed regardless), plus
- about $123,000 to $127,000 in WARN back pay (60 days at $750,000/yr, depending on whether calendar or working days are counted), plus
- the value of 60 days of health, dental, vision and retirement benefits, including any medical expenses she incurs that the plan would have covered, plus
- attorneys' fees to her as prevailing party (§ 2104(a)(6); § 1404), and a $500/day penalty on the local-government notice unless employees are paid within three weeks (§ 2104(a)(3); § 1403).

Thresholds are met: the October 2024 general WARN notice in HR Issues put 124 employees at the LA site with 65 affected, and Sarah's letter describes a similar multi-classification reduction. And the cost stays with Streams. APA Draft 2 § 2.3 excludes "Employment-related Liabilities, including WARN Act obligations" from what ParaZon assumes, and § 6.7 says "Seller is responsible for all WARN Act obligations." Because no deal has closed (the November 19 and 26, 2025 emails show negotiations still open, and the January 20, 2026 memo has Streams preparing to sue ParaZon), Streams is simply the employer ordering the layoff under § 2101(b)(1).

If you want WARN protection, the only two clean paths are (a) keep her on payroll with benefits intact through a separation date at least 60 days after a notice with a real date, or (b) add a separately stated, voluntary, unconditional payment that the notice expressly describes as not required by her agreement. Do not fold it into the contractual severance.

**2. The January 15, 2026 date is a term Streams is inventing**

Section 8(c) has no payment schedule, no release condition, and no deferral right. "Upon termination" is the natural reading. Announcing that she "will not receive [her] severance until January 15, 2026" is a unilateral modification without consideration, which is a breach-of-contract claim waiting to happen. Because the agreement calls the payment "salary," she can also argue it is wages due at discharge under Cal. Lab. Code § 201, with waiting-time penalties under § 203 of up to 30 days' pay (roughly $62,000 to $87,000 depending on the daily-rate method). The date also has no anchor: nothing happens on January 15 (no closing), it is fixed regardless of when she actually separates, and under Section III's 30 to 45 day rolling window it could fall before her separation. If you want a later date or a release, negotiate it with her for consideration; do not announce it.

Two housekeeping points on the agreement itself: the copy in the file is unsigned (blank signature blocks, and the effective date runs from the Employer's signature), so confirm an executed copy exists before pointing to it. And the document's numbering is off (Section 2 cross-references "Section 7 (Termination)" but termination is Section 8, and there is no Section 6), so cite 8(c) carefully.

**3. Whether Section IV accurately describes what she gets**

It undersells and omits:

- Amount and form. It never states $375,000 or whether it is a lump sum or six monthly installments of $62,500. The agreement is ambiguous on form; the notice should resolve it consistently with the agreement rather than with a made-up date.
- Bonus. Section 3 makes her eligible for performance bonuses "as determined by the Company." Say explicitly whether any bonus is payable, or she will claim an earned pro-rata bonus as wages.
- PTO. Section 4 gives 20 days a year. The "as reflected in timekeeping records" caveat is fine, but § 227.3 requires payout of all vested vacation at her final rate on the separation date.
- Health coverage. "Coverage will continue until the last day of the month ... unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier as a result of restructuring" is a problem twice over. If coverage ends inside the 60-day WARN period, the lost benefits and her medical bills become WARN damages. And APA Draft 2 § 2.2(d) keeps Streams' benefit plans out of the sale; if the plan is terminated, there is no plan for COBRA to continue, and the COBRA line becomes inaccurate.
