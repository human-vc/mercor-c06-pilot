**Bottom line**

Section IV does two things that hurt you. First, it locks in a set of payments none of which can be credited against WARN liability, so if the notice is found deficient (and as drafted, with bracketed dates and "may be impacted" language, it very likely will be), Streams pays the contractual severance and the full WARN award on top of it. Second, it misdescribes what Sarah is owed in two places: the January 15, 2026 severance date has no basis in her agreement, and the "unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier as a result of restructuring" carve-out cannot happen under the plan terms you gave me.

**1. WARN exposure: nothing in Section IV offsets it**

Both statutes in the file reduce an employer's back-pay liability only by three things: wages paid for the violation period, "voluntary and unconditional" payments "not required by any legal obligation," and third-party payments such as health premiums for the violation period (29 U.S.C. §2104(a)(2)(A)-(C), pp. 4-5 of the WARN Act PDF; Cal. Lab. Code §1402(c)(1)-(3)). Walking Section IV's items through that test:

- Final paycheck for wages earned through separation. These are wages for work already performed, not wages "for the period of the violation." No credit.
- Accrued PTO payout. Cal. Labor Code §1402(c)(1) expressly excludes "vacation moneys accrued prior to the period of the employer's violation," and it is independently owed under §227.3. No credit.
- COBRA information. Information is not a payment, and COBRA premiums are hers to pay. No credit.
- Severance "as outlined in your employment agreement." Section 8(c) of her agreement makes 6 months' salary ($375,000) mandatory on any termination without cause. A payment required by contract is by definition not "voluntary and unconditional" and not "not required by any legal obligation." Section IV's own wording documents that it is contractual, so it cannot be credited. Streams could not even fall back on calling it wages paid during the violation period, because Section IV defers it to January 15, 2026, which is after the 60-day window measured from the November 1, 2025 notice date (that window closes December 31, 2025). Cal. Labor Code §1402(c)(1) only credits wages "paid ... during the period of the employer's violation."
- Coverage through month-end. Streams' own premium payments for the days between separation and month-end are the one creditable item, and they are small. The restructuring carve-out, if ever exercised, would erase even that and add to liability, because the WARN award includes benefits and "the cost of any medical expenses incurred ... which would have been covered" (§2104(a)(1)(B); §1402(a)(2)).

What that means in dollars for Sarah alone: back pay for up to 60 days at her final rate of $750,000/yr is roughly $125,000 (two months' salary; the calendar-day method gives $123,288), plus the value of 60 days of health, dental and vision coverage and any uncovered medical expenses, plus attorney's fees (§2104(a)(6); §1404). That stacks on the $375,000 severance, which she gets regardless (§2105 says WARN remedies are in addition to contractual rights). The only cap is the smaller of 60 days or half her days of employment; her agreement carries no start date, so confirm she has been employed more than 120 days. The $500/day local-government penalty (§2104(a)(3); §1403) is separately waivable only if you pay each employee what WARN owes within three weeks of ordering the layoff; the January 15 severance date does nothing toward that either.

The APA drafts put all of this on Streams (Draft 2 §2.4(b) and §6.7 exclude WARN liabilities from what ParaZon assumes).

If you want payments to count against WARN, the routes are: keep her on payroll, working or not, through a properly noticed separation date 60 days out (salary paid during the period offsets dollar for dollar), or make a separate payment on top of the contractual severance that is voluntary, not conditioned on a release, and paid before the violation period ends. Reframing the existing severance will not work.

**2. Accuracy: what Section IV gets wrong about what she gets**

- The January 15, 2026 date is invented. Section 8(c) says "Upon termination without cause, the Employee shall receive 6 months salary." There is no deferral, no release condition, and no timing rule anywhere in the agreement (all six executive agreements in the file use identical language), and Section 11 makes the agreement the entire understanding. A unilateral deferral is a modification she never agreed to. It is also incoherent with the rest of the notice: her separation date is a blank placeholder that may move "earlier or later" across a 30-45 day rolling window, so the same fixed date is a two-month delay for a mid-November separation and a two-week delay for a late-December one. Separately, if the contractual severance is treated as wages under Labor Code §200 (arguable for a fixed contractual entitlement), a deferral past the termination date risks §203 waiting-time penalties of up to 30 days' wages, roughly $86,500 at her daily rate. Replace the sentence with the amount ($375,000, six months at $62,500) and the mechanics (lump sum or six monthly installments starting with the first regular payroll date after separation; the agreement is ambiguous on this, so pick one and state it).

- The benefits carve-out cannot be implemented. Under the plan terms you gave me, an active employee's coverage runs through the last day of the month in which employment ends, and eligibility changes take effect only through a formal amendment adopted under the plan's amendment procedure. "Restructuring" is not a plan amendment. The plan must be administered according to its written terms (ERISA §404(a)(1)(D)) and amended only through its stated procedure (§402(b)(3)). So the sentence misstates her entitlement, and if People Operations acts on it without an amendment you get a benefits claim, a larger WARN benefits component, and a shifted COBRA loss-of-coverage date that throws off the COBRA election notice timeline. Delete the carve-out and state plainly that health, dental and vision coverage continue through the last day of the month of separation, with COBRA (and Cal-COBRA) offered thereafter.

- Omissions worth closing. Section IV lists what she "will receive" but says nothing about performance bonuses (agreement §3 makes her bonus-eligible) or equity. The Equity Allocation Memo allocates 5% to the Head of Production and assumes the core creative team remains, and the Governance Summary has the Head of Production role continuing post-closing as a ParaZon appointee. If she holds any grant or accrued bonus, the list is incomplete, and the role-continuation point undercuts Section I's statement that her classification will not continue. The PTO line is fine on substance; just be aware that §227.3 requires payout of all vested vacation at her final rate regardless of what the timekeeping records show, and §201 requires final wages and PTO at termination.
