## Bottom line

Yes — Section IV creates real problems on both fronts you asked about. It (1) forfeits the only offsets that would have reduced our WARN damages, so we would pay the $375,000 severance **and** roughly $95,000–$123,000 of WARN back pay plus benefit costs, and (2) it misdescribes her benefits in two ways — a severance payment date we have no right to impose, and an open-ended right to cut off health coverage that the plan does not actually give us.

Facts I used: her employment agreement (§ 3 base salary $750,000/yr; § 8(c) "upon termination without cause, the Employee shall receive 6 months salary" = **$375,000**; § 4 PTO 20 days/yr); the notice is dated **Nov. 1, 2025** with separation "on or about [10–14 days]"; the roster shows 124 employees at the LA campus and the companion WARN notice shows 65 affected, so **both** federal WARN and Cal-WARN (Lab. Code § 1400 et seq.) are triggered.

## 1. What Section IV costs us under the WARN Act

**a. "Your severance will be as outlined in your employment agreement" destroys the offset.**
Cal. Lab. Code § 1402(c)(2) reduces WARN liability only for payments that are **"voluntary and unconditional … not required to satisfy any legal obligation."** Federal WARN, 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(2), is worded the same way. By expressly grounding the severance in her contract, Section IV documents that the $375,000 is a **legal obligation**, not a gratuitous payment — so it cannot be credited against back pay. That sentence is the single most expensive line in the letter: we pay severance *and* the full WARN measure. If we want creditable money, it has to be an *additional* payment, expressly voluntary and unconditional (i.e., not conditioned on a release).

**b. The January 15, 2026 deferral defeats the two remaining offsets.**
§ 1402(c)(1) credits **wages paid during the period of the violation**, and § 1402(c)(3) credits **premiums paid to a third party for health benefits during that period**. If she separates around Nov. 14, the violation window runs to roughly the end of December. A payment made **after** that window is over earns us no credit at all — even money that otherwise would have qualified. Worse, § 1403 waives the $500-per-day civil penalty only if the employer pays all § 1402 amounts **"within three weeks from the date the employer orders the mass layoff."** Holding $375,000 back until January blows that safe harbor for the entire laid-off group, not just Sarah.

**c. "Information regarding continuation of healthcare coverage under COBRA" understates what we owe.**
§ 1402(a)(2) makes us liable for **"the value of the cost of any benefits to which the employee would have been entitled had … employment not been lost, including the cost of any medical expenses incurred … that would have been covered."** That is the *employer-paid* cost of her medical/dental/vision for the violation period — not an offer of employee-paid COBRA. COBRA is the floor on continuation; it is not a substitute for benefit damages, and pointing at it in the notice invites the argument that we never intended to pay it.

**d. The quantified exposure.**

| Item | Basis | Approx. |
|---|---|---|
| Back pay, 46–50 days of violation (60 minus the 10–14 days the letter actually gives) | $750,000 ÷ 365 = $2,054.79/day | **$94,500 – $102,700** |
| If the notice is held invalid for vagueness (blanks, "may occur earlier") → full 60 days | $2,054.79 × 60 | **$123,300** |
| Benefits value for the violation period | employer share of medical/dental/vision, ~1.5–2 months | ~$3,500 – $6,000 (need actual premium) |
| Medical expenses she incurs that the plan would have covered | § 1402(a)(2) | open-ended — driven by the carve-out below |
| § 1403 civil penalty (agency notice) | $500/day × 46–60 | **$23,000 – $30,000** |
| Attorney's fees | § 1404 | TBD |
| Contractual severance — **not creditable** | § 8(c) | **$375,000** |

Rough all-in for Sarah alone: **about $495,000–$535,000**, versus roughly **$440,000–$480,000** if we had structured the severance and the notice period properly.

**e. No exception saves the short notice.** Cal-WARN's "actively seeking capital or business" exception (§ 1402.5) **expressly does not apply to a mass layoff** (§ 1402.5(d)), and in any event requires a state determination supported by a documentary record and a sworn affidavit. The letter's own admissions — "potential strategic transaction," "no final agreement has been executed," "additional classifications may be included" — are contemporaneous proof that we knew about this 60+ days out, which forecloses "unforeseeable business circumstances." And the rolling 30–45-day schedule in Section III does not help: separations aggregate over any 90-day window (29 U.S.C. § 2102(d)).

## 2. Does Section IV accurately describe what she gets?

**No, in four places.**

**a. The January 15, 2026 date is a unilateral change to her contract.** Section 8(c) entitles her to six months' salary on termination without cause, with **no deferral and no release condition**. Section IV opens with "**Upon separation, you will receive**" and then contradicts itself two sentences later. As drafted the letter is both (i) inaccurate and (ii) an anticipatory-breach exhibit. Exposure: breach of contract on $375,000 with interest; if the payment is characterized as wages, immediate-payment under Lab. Code § 201 and **waiting-time penalties under § 203 of up to 30 days at $2,054.79 = ~$61,600**. And because the money is already owed, we cannot lawfully demand a release as the price of paying it (pre-existing-duty problem; Lab. Code § 206.5 for anything that counts as wages) — so the deferral buys us nothing and costs us a leverage-free dispute.

**b. The severance is described, not quantified.** The letter never says the termination is **without cause**, which is the trigger for § 8(c); never states the amount; and never says whether "6 months salary" includes bonus. Under § 3, bonuses are discretionary, so the correct answer is $375,000 gross of base salary only — but silence leaves her free to claim six months of total compensation. It also omits any statement about the treatment of retirement plans or the § 4 benefits.

**c. The PTO line is qualified in a way California does not allow.** "As reflected in Streams' timekeeping records" is a unilateral cap on the measure. Her § 4 entitlement is 20 days a year; a PTO bank is treated as vested vacation and must be paid out at her **final rate of compensation** under Lab. Code § 227.3, and the records are evidence, not the entitlement. If the records understate her balance, this sentence is inaccurate and gives her a § 203 argument on top.

**d. The health-benefits sentence is the one you flagged, and it is wrong in both directions.**
- The first half is accurate: under the plan, an active employee's coverage runs through the last day of the month employment ends. If she separates in November, coverage runs to **November 30**, and we cannot take that back — it is a plan-based entitlement, and the letter tells her so.
- The second half — "**unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier as a result of restructuring**" — describes a power we do not have. Plan eligibility can change **only through a formal plan amendment adopted under the plan's amendment procedure**. "Restructuring" is not an amendment. So the carve-out is either (i) meaningless, in which case it is a misleading statement of her benefits that we will be charged with having made, or (ii) acted on, in which case the administrator is operating contrary to plan terms and she has a claim for benefits *and* for the resulting medical bills, which § 1402(a)(2) lets her recover as WARN damages. It also cuts off our § 1402(c)(3) credit for premiums paid during the violation period — so early termination of coverage **increases** net WARN damages dollar for dollar.
- It is also internally inconsistent with the COBRA line: a mid-month cutoff creates a gap in which she is neither an active participant nor yet facing a clean COBRA qualifying event, and if the plan itself is wound down at closing there may be no COBRA obligation to describe at all.

**e. One collateral point worth deciding now.** Her § 7 non-compete/non-solicit is void in California (B&P Code § 16600; §§ 16600.1 and 16600.5, and SB 699), and it is unlawful to include or attempt to enforce it. Section IV promises her severance "as outlined in your employment agreement" — an agreement that purports to restrain her for a year. Do not condition the severance on § 7 compliance, and confirm whether the individualized § 16600.5 notice that such clauses are void has gone out to her; that is independent UCL exposure separate from WARN.

## 3. Recommended fixes to Section IV

1. **Delete the January 15 date.** Pay the $375,000 gross (less withholding) at separation, on the contract's terms, and state the amount and the payment date. If we want a release, it needs **new consideration above** the contractual amount plus OWBPA timing (45 days + 7-day revocation if she is 40+) — and the release must not be the thing that makes the WARN payment "conditional," or we lose § 1402(c)(2) credit.
2. **Confirm expressly that the termination is without cause**, so § 8(c) is unambiguous, and state that severance is **in addition to** any WARN amounts owed.
3. **Fix the benefits sentence:** "Your group health coverage ends November 30, 2025, consistent with plan terms; Streams will pay the employer share of your premiums through [end of the notice period]; COBRA election materials will follow separately." Delete "unless changes in plan eligibility occur earlier." Any earlier cutoff requires a **board/administrator-adopted plan amendment** first — do not act without it.
4. **State her actual PTO balance and the payout rate** rather than deferring to the timekeeping records.
5. **Fix the timing that drives the whole number.** Re-issue with a real date at least 60 days out, keep her on payroll (or paid leave) through the notice period — wages paid during the period are creditable under § 1402(c)(1) and premiums under § 1402(c)(3) — and serve the EDD/local workforce board/elected-official notices at the same time, then pay any § 1402 amounts within three weeks to kill the § 1403 penalty.
6. **Fill the blanks and drop the "may occur earlier" language** in Sections I and III; a notice that reserves the right to separate sooner than noticed is not a valid WARN notice, and the unfilled "[insert date range]" placeholders are a standalone § 1401(b) content defect (29 C.F.R. § 639.7 requires the expected separation date and a named company official).

**Two cross-references outside Section IV that you should not miss:** APA **Draft 1 § 4.3** requires Seller to deliver Cal-WARN notices "at least five (5) days before Closing" — that covenant is flatly incompatible with § 1401's 60 days and, if signed, hard-wires the violation; and **Draft 1 § 4.2** has *Buyer* terminating at closing while § 2.1(b) has Buyer assuming employment obligations for employees not offered jobs, whereas **Draft 2 §§ 2.3 and 6.7** push all WARN liability back onto Streams. Under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(b)(1) the seller is responsible through the effective date of the sale and the buyer thereafter, so the current mismatch leaves Streams holding Sarah's claim with no indemnity. Also note that Draft 1's representation that Seller is "in full compliance with all labor and employment laws" is contradicted by a 10–14-day notice, which converts an employment problem into a disclosure/indemnity problem with ParaZon.

**Assumptions I made** (tell me if any is wrong and I will re-run the numbers): separation on or about Nov. 14, 2025; $750,000 is her final rate of compensation; she has been employed more than 120 days (so the § 1402(b) half-the-days cap does not bite); and the violation period is measured in calendar days. To firm up the benefits line I need the employer's monthly share of her medical/dental/vision premium and her current PTO balance.
